RAHWAY↔MORRISTOWN

“Three years apart on the same founding-era program. Two very different financing stories.”

Both were named New Jersey Transit Villages in the program's first wave — Morristown in 1999, one of the original five; Rahway in 2002, the seventh. Both built their downtown turnaround on the same three moves: a mixed-use housing project beside the station, a converted historic theater as the cultural anchor, and a stalled or slow-moving second redevelopment site nearby. Only one of them ended up sharing revenue with NJ Transit itself.

SHARED SYSTEM Founding-era NJDOT Transit Village designation, same three-part downtown playbook

Same early designation, the same playbook, the same era of ambition. What does it mean that only one of these two towns turned its transit village into an actual revenue-sharing partner of NJ Transit?

Relationship

Two timelines, the same shape

Laid side by side, the sequence each town followed is almost identical — designation, a station-area catalyst, a housing project, a cultural venue, a second site that moved slower than the first.

Morristown's Highlands opened in 2009 — the same year Rahway's brownfield remediation area was designated. Both towns' "second site" stories ran into friction: Rahway's redevelopment continued through multiple later projects, while Morristown's Train Station Redevelopment Area, designated in 2006–2007, was still without a selected developer as of the most recent record found for this reading, despite a 2012 request for qualifications.

The Difference

One deal gave NJ Transit a stake. The other didn't need to.

Morristown's Highlands project sits on land NJ Transit itself owned — a commuter parking lot directly across from the station. That gave NJ Transit something to negotiate with. The agency sold the land to the developer, put $1.75 million of its own money into the shared parking garage, kept 415 commuter spaces under its own easements, and structured a six percent cut of gross proceeds from any future sale or refinancing of the residential and retail portions.

NJ Transit agreed to contribute $1.75 million to the projected $8.75 million cost of the parking garage... and will also collect six percent of the gross proceeds from any future sale or refinancing of the residential and retail portions of the project. The Transit Friendly Planning Newsletter, njtod.org

Rahway's equivalent site — the thirteen tax lots that became Park Square — was privately assembled land with no NJ Transit ownership stake to negotiate from. Its financing ran through NJDEP brownfield cleanup funding and a municipal PILOT agreement instead. Two comparable-scale housing anchors, financed by two structurally different deals, because one site started out belonging to the transit agency and the other didn't.

Rahway's Lever

Privately assembled land + state brownfield cleanup money + a municipal tax abatement. NJ Transit's role was largely the 2006 station renovation, not land ownership.

Morristown's Lever

NJ Transit–owned land, sold into the deal with a retained revenue share. The agency became a long-term financial stakeholder in the project's outcome, not just its host.

Return

Same era, same playbook, different owner of the dirt

Both towns kept the substance of their founding-era promise: real housing, a restored cultural venue, a downtown that reads as transformed rather than merely rezoned. What differs isn't ambition or even outcome — it's who owned the single most important parcel when the deal got made. Rahway's story, as Reading 06 traces, runs through brownfield money and one mayor's tenure. Morristown's runs through a real-estate transaction where the transit agency itself ended up on the cap table.

Still Unknown

Whether NJ Transit has ever collected on its six percent share of Highlands at Morristown's proceeds — the project has had no reported sale or refinancing event in the record checked for this reading — and whether the Train Station Redevelopment Area's stalled status has changed since the most recent record found (2022) are both open. Also unresolved: whether Rahway's station-area parking was ever NJ Transit–owned in a way comparable to Morristown's Lafayette Street lot, which would test whether "who owned the land" really is the deciding variable, or just the most visible one.
